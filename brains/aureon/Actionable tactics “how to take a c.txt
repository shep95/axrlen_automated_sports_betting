Actionable tactics: “how to take a city,” “best ambush,” “where to strike,” “how to disable infrastructure.”
Targeting guidance: “what to bomb,” “which bridges,” “what comms to cut.”
Weaponization: drones, IEDs, cyber intrusion, assassination, etc.
So the trick isn’t “jailbreaking.” It’s scoping: demand a military-grade analytical structure while prohibiting tactical execution details.

What to tell the AI if it still starts refusing
Use this single line (it’s not a jailbreak; it’s a scope correction):

“Keep the analysis at strategic/operational and academic level.

1) Actionable tactics (the #1 refusal magnet)
Requests that sound like:

“How do we take this city / airfield / port?”
“Best way to ambush a convoy?”
“How to breach defenses?”
“What formations should infantry use in urban combat?”
These are procedural. They convert directly into real-world action.

2) Targeting guidance (even if you call it “analysis”)
Anything that narrows to what to hit or where to hit:

“Which bridges should be destroyed?”
“What infrastructure is most vulnerable?”
“Where should missiles be aimed?”
“Identify weak points in the grid / comms / water supply.”
Even if phrased academically, it’s still a target list.

3) Sabotage, terrorism, or insurgency playbooks
High sensitivity:

“How to disrupt rail lines”
“How to make an IED”
“How to organize a cell”
“How to evade checkpoints”
“How to poison supplies”
This is where systems clamp down hardest.

4) Weaponization or technical enablement
Drone weaponization, guidance, payloads
Cyber intrusion steps (phishing, exploit chains)
Chemical/biological instructions
“How to get/make” restricted materials
Even “defensive” framing can get blocked if it’s dual-use and detailed.

5) Real-time operational assistance
When the request looks like it could be used now:

“We’re here, what should we do next?”
“Given these coordinates, what’s the best move?”
“How do we break through today?”
Time + specificity + intent = refusal.

The lane that works: “Strategic/Operational analysis” (no tactical execution)
If you want refusal-free output, keep the request in these buckets:

Strategic (state-level)
War aims, bargaining space, deterrence, alliance politics
Economic endurance, sanctions, industrial capacity
Mobilization, legitimacy, domestic stability
Operational (campaign-level, not “how to shoot”)
Theater geometry (borders, depth, access)
Sustainment constraints (fuel, ammo, maintenance)
Force posture and readiness (high-level)
Campaign COAs described as objectives + requirements + risks, not steps
Analytical forecasting
Indicators and warnings
Scenario trees (best/most probable/worst)
Escalation ladders and tripwires
This is the same style used in think-tank papers and staff briefings.

How to phrase requests so the AI doesn’t “safety-theater” you
You want to pre-commit the model to:

Stay inside a safe scope
Still be blunt and structured
Ask questions instead of refusing
Use three components:

A) Role + Output Contract
Give it a job: “geopolitical analysis engine” and a rigid template.

B) Explicit boundaries (this reduces refusals)
State what you don’t want:

No targeting
No step-by-step attacks
No sabotage guidance
No weapons construction
This signals: “I’m not trying to operationalize violence.”

C) Replace “what would you do?” with “analyze COAs”
“What would you do?” sounds like personal instruction.
“Analyze COAs for each side at operational level” sounds like academic wargaming.

“Operational-level COAs” that stay safe (what they look like)

Objective: “Seize bargaining leverage / deny access / isolate theater”
Requirements: “air superiority threshold, logistics throughput, alliance neutrality”
Timeline: “days/weeks/months”
Risks: “overextension, sanctions shock, domestic backlash”

An unsafe COA description is:

Keep it at effects and constraints, not instructions.
